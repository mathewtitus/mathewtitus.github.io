Exercise 1.1

\(M\) being locally euclidean means for each point \(p\in M\) we can find
  • an open set \(U\) in \(M\) containing \(p\)
  • an open set \(\tilde{U}\) in \(\mathbb{R}^n\)
  • a homeomorphism \(\phi: U \to \tilde{U}\)
Show one arrives at an equivalent definition if we replace \(\tilde{U}\) with an open ball in \(\mathbb{R}^n\) or all of \(\mathbb{R}^n\) itself. Proof:
Assume the original definition (loc-euc); refer to the other definitions as (ball-loc-euc) and (plane-loc-euc), respectively. By restricting the set \(\tilde{U}\) to an open ball \(B \subset \tilde{U} \subset \mathbb{R}^n\), we have \(U' := \phi^{-1}(B)\) is open in \(M\). Hence using \(\phi\vert_{U'}\) we have a homeomorphism on an open set about \(p\) mapping to an open ball. Then any loc-euc space \(M\) is also ball-loc-euc.
Let \(r\) be the euclidean distance to the center, \(c\), of the open ball \( B = B(c,r_B)\) and let \(\psi\) be the function \(\psi:[0,r_B) \to [0,\infty) \) given by \(\psi(r):= \tan(\pi/2r_B) \). Then the map \(\Psi\) taking \(x = c + r{x - c \over |x - c|}\in B\) to \(\Psi(x) = c + \psi(r){x - c \over |x-c|}\) is a homeomorphism from the ball \(B\) to all of euclidean \(n\)-space. Hence, because composition preserves the homeomorphic property of maps, the ball-loc-euc space is also (using the map \(\Psi \circ \phi\)) plane-loc-euc, and vice versa.
Finally, if we take a ball-loc-euc space, the image \(B\) of the homeomorphism \(\phi\) is a fortiori an open set, and so the original loc-euc definition is upheld whenever \(M\) is ball-loc-euc about each point \(p \in M\).

Exercise 1.2

Show that any topological subspace of a Hausdorff space \(M\) is Hausdorff, and any finite prooduct of Hausdorff spaces is Hausdorff. Proof:
(i) Let \(p, q\) be two points in the subspace. Then there exist \(U, V \subset M\) so that \(p \in U\) and \(q \in V \) and \(U \cap V = \emptyset\). If \(N\) is the subspace then \(U \cap N\) and \(V \cap N\) are two open subsets of \(N\) satisfying the required conditions.
(ii) If \(M_1, \dots, M_n \) are Hausdorff spaces, and \( (p_1, \dots, p_n), (q_1, \dots, q_n) \) are points in the product space \(\prod M_i \), then there exist for all \(i = 1, \dots, n\) disjoint sets \(U_i, V_i \subset M_i\) so that \(p_i \in U_i\) and \(q_i \in V_i\). Since \(\prod_i U_i \) and \(\prod_i V_i\) are open subsets of the product topology, they satisfy the required conditions on \( p = (p_1, \dots, p_n) \) and \(q = (q_1, \dots, q_n) \).

Exercise 1.3

Show that any topological subspace of a second countable space is second countable, and any finite product of second countable spaces is second countable. Proof:
Let \(M\) be the space, \(N\) the subspace. One can verify that the projection of a topological basis \(\mathcal{B}\) to a subspace \(N\) (i.e. \( \{U \cap N \vert U \in \mathcal{B} \} \)) is again a basis.
Similarly, for spaces \(M_i\) with bases \(\mathcal{B}_i\), the product of elements from each basis forms a basis for the product space. Yes, I'm being a bit lazy here. The easy way to check these statements is to use the definition of a basis as a set of open sets such that any point in an intersection of two basis elements is again contained in a basis element that is a subset of the intersection of those same two basis elements.
