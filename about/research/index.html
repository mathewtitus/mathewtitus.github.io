Research Interests

By Mathew Titus

April 4, 2024

These are, in broad strokes, a number of questions that I would like to gain a deeper understanding of. It is not a claim that the answers aren’t out there; they may well be. But for me the search for some bedrock intuition and understanding is ongoing.

One caveat: these items have not had a QA pass to make sure they’re fit for human consumption, so if they appear opaque, it is because they are. Hopefully clarity will come with more work and more writing.

1. Dependence in the Central Limit Theorem and Concentration of Measure

In one of its incarnations, the Central Limit Theorem (CLT) states that a random projection of a centered, finite variance \(n\)-fold product measure approaches a gaussian distribution as \(n \to \infty\). Similarly, a classic concentration of measure result states that the uniform distribution on a \(n\)-dimensional hypersphere of radius \(\sqrt{n}\) will project to a near-gaussian distribution, \((2\pi)^{-1/2} \exp(-x^2/2)\).

The latter is a fact about high-dimensional geometry: projecting (uniform distributions over) shapes that are “regular enough” leads to a normal distribution. The former is a fundamental fact of probability spaces. It would be beautiful to expand the concentration of measure result to a general family of geometric objects, show that product probability measures with low dependence among the dimensions (i.e. the components of the vector \(x\) being sampled are nearly independent) belong to this family, and thus the CLT will apply.

One would hope that the geometric family in question may turn out to be broader than the collection of product measure that known CLTs apply to.

2. The relation of Time to Entropy

In his beautiful, lucid book, “The Order of Time,” Carlo Rovelli describes what we know about time via experimentation: it is not well-ordered (that is, although some events definitely precede others, there are many events where one cannot be said to come before the other); it does not flow uniformly in all places; the “present” only makes sense on very small scales; relative motion distorts the flow of time between objects; and with one exception (the second law of thermodynamics) our physical laws are agnostic to the direction of its flow. Rovelli argues that the experience of time and its passing is a phenomenon fueled by entropy.

This is very compelling - that the interactions that we see changing the universe around us are only “occurring” in the sense that given a point in space and time, there is a direction in which the universe’s entropy is increasing, and a mind (or anything else) can only carry information in this direction. To go the other way would be to live in a world where particular arrangements of particles and exchanges of energy drive the system into specialized states with no relation to earlier states. In this scenario, the firing of neurons in the brain would also be happening with no regard for the “prior” state, so such a brain would not be able to experience a persistent flow of time along this temporal path. In a sense, everything that has and will happen exists, but we are, right now, experiencing a certain slice through that record of happenings; the sequence of events along this direction of time is also the sequence which allows us to have persistent systems (bodies, minds) that carry information from one set of happenings (the particular “past”) to another set of happenings (the higher entropy future).

It is fun to contemplate, almost as sport, the manner in which we might lift Lagrangian and Hamiltonian dynamics, universally indexed by some notion of time, out of their dependence on a unique, universal time coordinate. Instead, we index events by an entropy-governed notion of past / future (or dispense with the notion entirely). While I don’t have any concrete leads, Rovelli points to a body of literature on heat-time that explores this unique subfield.

3. Dimensionality of the Universe

Why are there three spatial dimensions? While we’ve moved away from models of the universe that have three euclidean/cartesian space coordinates and a single universal time coordinate, the modern Riemannian geometries that solve Einstein’s field equations are still studied in order to solve for systems with 3+1 spacetime dimensions.

While our personal experience of the world clearly seems to occupy such a universe, the fundamental physical laws of the standard particle physics model (quantum electroweak and quantum chromodynamics theories) can be formulated easily in other numbers of dimensions. Thought experiments along the lines of Mach’s principle suggest to me that the universe ought to display a local, emergent dimensionality. Experimental anomalies also suggest that our idea of a locally isotropic, fixed dimensional universe may not be well founded (see the failure of C-symmetry).
